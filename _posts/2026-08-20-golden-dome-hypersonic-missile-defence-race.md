Hypersonic weapons — missiles that fly faster than Mach 5 and, in the case of glide vehicles and aeroballistic designs, manoeuvre unpredictably along the way — have spent the last few years moving from a theoretical problem to one that missile-defence budgets are now built around. 2026 is shaping up as the year several of the resulting defensive programmes stop being slideware.

## The US bet: Golden Dome

The most consequential move is the **Golden Dome for America** initiative, introduced by the Trump administration in January 2025 with an estimated cost of **$185 billion over ten years**, according to Golden Dome Director Gen. Michael Guetlein. In April 2026, the US Space Force awarded **$3.2 billion across 20 Other Transaction Authority (OTA) agreements to twelve companies**, funding early competitive development rather than a single locked-in design — and boost-phase space-based interceptor prototypes were separately awarded in November 2025, with kinetic midcourse (hit-to-kill) interceptor awards expected in February 2026.

Underpinning all of it is a tracking problem as hard as the interception problem: hypersonic glide vehicles are dim, fast, and manoeuvring, which is why the **Space Development Agency's Tracking Layer** — moving through Tranche 0 (launched February 2024) toward later tranches running through 2030, and a further 72-satellite Tranche 3 tracking layer beginning in 2029 — sits at the centre of the architecture, alongside the dedicated **Hypersonic and Ballistic Tracking Space Sensor (HBTSS)** prototypes launched by Northrop Grumman and L3Harris in 2024 for a two-year evaluation.

Existing naval systems are being upgraded in parallel: the Navy's **AEGIS Sea Based Terminal (SBT) Increment 3** pairs an enhanced-guidance SM-6 Block IA missile against hypersonic glide vehicles, while the US-Japan co-developed **Glide Phase Interceptor (GPI)** — which received a $475 million Northrop Grumman contract modification in April 2026 — is targeting full operational capability in the early 2030s.

## Europe is building its own layer

Europe isn't waiting on Washington. The EU's PESCO-backed **TWISTER** initiative is targeting fielding around 2035, centred on space-based surveillance, sensor data fusion and a multi-role interceptor. **ODIN's EYE II**, a 38-partner industry consortium led by Germany's OHB System AG, is running a 36-month study through late 2026 on space-based hypersonic tracking. A Spanish-German consortium, **HYDEF**, completed its concept phase in October 2026 around a highly manoeuvrable interceptor with advanced seeker technology, while **HYDIS2** — a France-Germany-Italy-Netherlands collaboration — is aiming for a finalised design by 2030 and in-service status by 2035.

## The takeaway

None of this is close to solved. Every serious programme in this space is still running on a 2030s-plus timeline, which tells you as much about how hard the intercept problem is as it does about how seriously governments are now funding it. What's changed in 2026 isn't that the problem's been cracked — it's that the money behind trying to crack it has become very real, very quickly.
