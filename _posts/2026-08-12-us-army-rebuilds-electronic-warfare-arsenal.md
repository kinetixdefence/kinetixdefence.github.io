Two flagship US Army electronic warfare programmes have been quietly abandoned — and what's replacing them tells you a lot about how the Army now thinks it needs to fight in the electromagnetic spectrum.

## What got cancelled, and why

According to Breaking Defense, the Army scrapped both **MFEW-Air Large**, its airborne electronic warfare programme, and **TLS-EAB**, its ground-based system, after concluding both had become victims of a "monolithic acquisition approach" — large, slow, single-platform programmes that got expensive and outdated before they ever fielded. One Army colonel involved is quoted describing how the ground system "became very expensive" after three years of development without reaching the field.

## The replacement approach: smaller, faster, modular

In their place, the Army is pursuing a new airborne jammer programme — reported as a "new start" for 2026 — and a lightweight ground-based EW system now in prototyping, expected to run over the next one to two years. Both are being built around a deliberately different philosophy: modular, open-architecture designs using commercial off-the-shelf technology rather than bespoke, exquisite hardware, integrated with the Army's Launched Effects minidrones and combining free-flying and tethered drones, ground antennas and distributed operators.

The stated goals mirror the same trend showing up across land-warfare procurement generally: real-time visualisation of the electromagnetic spectrum, and physically separating human operators from the emitting equipment that gives away their position — a direct response to how effectively adversaries can now geolocate and target anything that broadcasts.

## Why this matters beyond the US Army

The pattern here — abandon the large, slow, exquisite platform in favour of smaller, faster-iterating, networked systems — is showing up across multiple defence-technology categories in 2026, from drones to armoured vehicles. Electronic warfare may be the clearest case yet: the Army is explicitly trading platform sophistication for fielding speed, betting that a system it can update every year or two beats one it spent a decade perfecting and still couldn't afford to deploy.
