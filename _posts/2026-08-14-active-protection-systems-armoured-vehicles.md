The defining design problem in land warfare right now isn't armour thickness. It's reaction time.

Loitering munitions, first-person-view drones and shoulder-fired anti-tank weapons have made the modern battlefield lethal to armoured vehicles in a way traditional steel and composite plating was never built to survive. The industry's answer, increasingly standard on new and upgraded platforms, is the **active protection system (APS)**: radar-cued, computer-controlled hardware that detects an incoming threat and destroys it in mid-air, in milliseconds, before it reaches the hull.

## How it actually works

Systems like **Trophy** — developed by Israel's Rafael Advanced Defense Systems and Elta Group (a subsidiary of Israel Aerospace Industries), and integrated onto platforms including the US Army's M1A2 Abrams by General Dynamics Land Systems and Leonardo DRS — use 360-degree radar to track an incoming rocket-propelled grenade or anti-tank guided missile, calculate an intercept point, then fire a small shaped charge from a gimbal-mounted launcher to destroy it before impact. The same radar picture can also flag the shooter's position back to the crew.

It's a genuinely hard engineering problem: the system has to distinguish a real threat from clutter, compute a firing solution, and act, all within a window measured in milliseconds — which is why the bottleneck isn't the interceptor charge, it's processor speed and radar miniaturisation.

## Where it's headed next

Two things are pulling APS development forward in 2026. First, the threat picture has widened well past RPGs and ATGMs — loitering munitions and cheap FPV drones now feature in the same threat briefings, and current-generation hard-kill systems are optimised for faster, more predictable projectiles, not slow, manoeuvring quadcopters. Second, vehicle manufacturers are redesigning around the assumption that any future vehicle will absorb some hits: at Eurosatory 2026 in Paris, International Armored Group (IAG) showcased its Rila Xtreme and Guardian Xtreme protected-vehicle lines built around blast-dissipating monocoque hulls and modular mission packages, explicitly framed around a threat set that now includes "artillery fragments, drones, loitering munitions, and anti-tank ambushes" rather than mines and IEDs alone.

Industry and defence-press analysis is candid that today's systems remain unproven against the hardest cases — TOW-class missiles, helicopter-launched weapons, and any future hypersonic threat — which is why continued investment in faster processing and more compact radar is treated as a standing requirement rather than a solved problem.

## Why it matters

Active protection is shifting from a premium add-on fitted to a handful of flagship tanks to something closer to a baseline expectation across armoured fleets — a direct, measurable response to how cheap, precision-guided threats have changed the economics of land warfare.
