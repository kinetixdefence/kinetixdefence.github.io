If one platform sums up where the US Navy's money is going in 2026, it's the submarine — not the aircraft carrier that has anchored American naval identity for eighty years.

## What's been ordered

Stars and Stripes reported that the Navy has awarded contracts for **14 new attack and ballistic-missile submarines**, and Naval News separately described the service's released shipbuilding plan as going "all in" on the undersea fleet relative to surface combatants. Alongside new construction, the Navy is investing in production speed itself: a separate Army Recognition report describes an AI-driven system being deployed specifically to **accelerate manufacturing of the Columbia-class and Virginia-class submarine programmes**, both of which have struggled for years with build-rate delays relative to programme targets.

## Why submarines, and why now

The strategic logic, per Naval News' coverage of the Navy's undersea-warfare push, centres on what officials describe as **"seabed superiority"** — contesting the ocean floor itself, not just the water column, as adversary undersea-cable-tapping, seabed-warfare and deep-sensor capabilities mature. Submarines remain the one major surface-or-subsurface asset that's genuinely difficult for a peer adversary to track and target at range, which matters directly in the context of a well-documented broader US strategic focus on deterring and, if necessary, operating against China in the Indo-Pacific.

## The production bottleneck is the real story

The more interesting story here isn't the order size, it's the acknowledgement that the US submarine-industrial base has struggled to build boats fast enough — hence a dedicated AI production-acceleration tool getting deployed specifically against the Columbia and Virginia lines, rather than treating shipyard throughput as a problem money alone will fix. Expect submarine-industrial-base capacity, not weapons capability, to remain the metric worth watching through the rest of this decade.
