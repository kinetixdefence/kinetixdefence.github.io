India's Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) is in the middle of a deliberate policy shift: handing its own missile and platform technologies to private Indian industry, rather than keeping development and production locked inside government labs.

## What's actually been handed over

In a formal ceremony on **18 August 2026**, DRDO's Combat Vehicles Research & Development Establishment (CVRDE) in Chennai transferred three specific technologies to industry and armed-forces partners: a co-axial lube oil filter assembly, a mainshaft bearing component, and indigenous filter elements for MiG-29K/KUB naval fighter aircraft. The handover — confirmed by an official Press Information Bureau release — involved coordination between CVRDE, the Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE), certification bodies CEMILAC and DGAQA, and three private manufacturing partners: Mikroflo Filters (Hyderabad), Amado Tools (Bangalore), and Austin Engineering Company (Gujarat). Distinguished Scientist Prof. Prateek Kishore, serving as chief guest, said the transfer reflected India's capability "to build world-class defence equipment," while stressing the importance of timely delivery of critical technologies through close stakeholder engagement.

## The bigger policy move: opening up missile tech

That handover sits inside a much larger shift reported separately: the Indian government has moved to open its **conventional DRDO missile technologies to Indian private industry** more broadly, a policy direction aimed at building genuine indigenous production capacity for systems that were previously the exclusive domain of government labs and public-sector defence firms.

## Why this matters beyond India

This is a specific instance of a pattern showing up across multiple rising defence-industrial powers: the shift from "government lab designs, government factory builds" toward "government lab designs, licenses to private industry, industry scales production" — the same model that underpins much of the US and European defence-industrial base, and one India has historically lagged on due to a heavily public-sector-dominated defence manufacturing history. Whether this translates into faster fielding and export competitiveness, or gets slowed by the practical friction of technology transfer between government labs and private firms, is the thing worth watching over the next few years.
