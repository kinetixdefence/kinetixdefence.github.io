Powered exoskeletons for soldiers have been "five years away" for most of the last two decades. What's different in 2026 is that the conversation has moved from research demonstrators to formal industry solicitations.

## Why now

The renewed push is driven by a straightforward physical problem: the modern dismounted infantry load — body armour, ammunition, batteries, sensors, communications gear — has climbed steadily for years, to the point where fatigue itself has become a tactical liability, not just a quality-of-life issue. Industry coverage describes militaries formally asking manufacturers for exoskeleton systems aimed squarely at soldier performance and endurance rather than novelty demonstrations, and defence-strategy commentary is increasingly treating exoskeletons alongside other "future soldier" technologies — better protective materials, improved physiological monitoring — as part of one connected modernisation effort rather than isolated gadgets.

## What it's realistically for

The credible near-term use case is unglamorous: reducing fatigue and injury on long foot patrols and load-carrying tasks, not turning individual soldiers into powered-armour super-infantry. Passive, unpowered exosuits that redistribute load mechanically are closer to fielding than fully powered, battery-driven systems, which still face hard constraints around battery weight, noise, and reliability in the field — the same constraints that have slowed this technology category for years.

## Worth treating with caution

This is a category where we'd flag more caution than most of our coverage: much of the available reporting is industry-facing (trade press covering what vendors are pitching to procurement officers) rather than independently verified operational data, and concrete numbers — how many units, what actual endurance gains, what timeline to fielding — are not consistently available across sources.
